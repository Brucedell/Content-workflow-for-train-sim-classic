1 · Setting up the storefront provider

Part 1 recommended a hosted creator store (route A) for the launch. Whichever one you choose, the set-up is the same seven steps and takes an afternoon.

  1. Account and payout. Sign up under the studio name; connect the bank account or PayPal; fill in the tax form (W-9 for a U.S. seller). Switch on any “collect sales tax / VAT for me” option: that makes the provider the merchant of record. check
  2. Store page branding. Upload the logo and set the accent colour so it matches the landing site; set the store address to store.yourstudio (Part 5 §3).
  3. One product per vehicle or route. Title in the design-name form (§6), the same images as the product page, the short pitch, the price. Mark it a digital product.
  4. Upload the file (§2). Providers keep every file you upload; use the versioned name so you can tell them apart later.
  5. Licence text. Paste your EULA (one page: personal use, no redistribution of the paid files, textures open for community repaints, no refunds after download except where law requires).
  6. Discount codes and bundles. Create a launch code and, when you have two related products, a bundle; both are a few clicks.
  7. Test purchase. Set the price to $0 or use the provider's test mode, buy it yourself, follow the e-mail, download, install. Then set the real price.

The readme is the support ticket you never get

Five lines: which game version it needs, how to install, where the manual is, what to send when it breaks (TSC: the LogMate log; TF3: stdout.txt), and the version. Put the same five lines on the product page.

3 · Shipping updates without breaking anyone

  • Never rename blueprint paths, the TF3 mod folder suffix, or the product's provider ID; these are what saves, scenarios and libraries point at.
  • Keep the previous zip attached to the product for a month so anyone whose scenario broke can roll back.
  • Batch fixes into a patch every two to four weeks unless something is unplayable; frequent tiny updates train buyers to ignore update mail.
  • Changelog in plain words (“pantograph no longer clips the roof in the cab view”), not internal names.

5 · Pre-orders, greenlight thresholds and memberships

The opportunity analysis proposes two funding models that both start from the interactive viewer. Both are built from features the storefront providers already have (pre-orders, memberships), so they are configuration, not code.

The golden rule from the analysis

Launch any pre-order with a working proof: the free “Proving Ground” demo route or a free demo vehicle that shows the scripted systems running in the real game. The interactive web model proves the art; the demo proves the code. Backers trust what they have already driven.

6 · Names, logos, liveries: the legal playbook

Part 2 §5 gave the naming table. The reasoning, in one figure, so you can apply it to a product the table does not list.

  • Product title form: Class / Model + designation + type, e.g. “Class EF-4 ‘Little Joe’ Heavy Electric”, “Model M-10000 Early Streamliner”.
  • Ship the model in a plausible unlettered scheme (builder's photo grey, or a plain colour) with the texture folders open; a day after release the community will have the real ones.
  • Routes: describe the place and era (“Texas interurban, 1930s, street running”); street names and buildings are fine, business logos are not.
  • Screenshots on the store are of your unlettered version. Community repaints in screenshots invite questions.
  • If a studio partnership (official DLC on Steam) comes up later, you license the compiled product; keep the master .blend, textures and source scripts yours. The analysis covers the split in detail.

7 · The money: fees, taxes, what you keep

Planning figures from the analysis for one $40 flagship route, before any pre-order income. They assume a hosted store taking ~10% all-in and a Texas-based sole proprietor (0% state income tax).

Self-employment + federal income tax (~27–28%)−$4,632−$11,705−$24,842
Take-home$11,868$29,995$63,658
  • Self-employment tax (Social Security + Medicare) is 15.3% on 92.35% of profit; federal income tax about 15% effective at these levels; a state with income tax would take a further 6–10%.
  • Set aside 30% of every payout in a separate account the day it lands; pay quarterly estimated tax. This single habit is the difference between a business and a surprise.
  • Hours matter more than units: the analysis works out ~$15/h at Mid and ~$32/h at High for a 2,000-hour route. A $15–25 locomotive built on reused scripts and bogies is a far better hour.
  • Fees are the price of not running checkout, tax and delivery yourself. Moving to Shopify + Stripe (route B) at scale lowers the percentage but adds monthly plans and apps; revisit at 10+ products.
  • Expenses you can deduct: Blender is free, but the games, DLC used for testing, reference books, the domain, hosting, a share of the PC. Keep receipts in one folder.
Steam instead?

Official DLC via a publisher means 30% to Valve plus the publisher's share, but visibility and automatic updates. The analysis's position: independent first, with the design-name catalogue and your own customer list; a partnership later is additive as long as you only license the compiled product.

8 · The e-mail list

The analysis calls this the first of three leverage points, and it costs nothing: every buyer and every demo downloader gives you an address. The storefront provider collects buyers' addresses by itself; for the free demo, use the provider's $0 product so the download goes through the same door. When product two launches, one mail reaches everyone who already trusts product one.

  • One list, one message per release and one per major update; a dev-log digest at most monthly.
  • Export the list from the provider every quarter (it is your customer list, not theirs).
  • Put the sign-up on the landing page footer and the dev log; a sentence, not a pop-up.
